\begin{abstract}
Biochar is among the most scalable engineered carbon dioxide removal (CDR)
pathways, but its economics depend on carbon accounting rather than energy
sales, while existing models treat carbon policy as exogenous. We present a
multi-product biochar supply chain model for Wisconsin (72 counties, 13
feedstocks, two pyrolysis temperatures, price-responsive demand) in the
market-clearing framework of Sampat et al.~\cite{sampat2019}, with
mass-balance duals as spatial equilibrium prices. We embed
baseline-and-credit, cap-and-trade, and tiered-tax policy architectures, the
last with H/C-gated VM0044-inspired removal crediting. The no-policy
equilibrium yields 2.30~Mt biochar/yr, \$708~M/yr surplus, and 1.60~Mt
CO$_2$e/yr net removal. A linear carbon tax leaves this optimum unchanged,
and net emission caps bind only below the system's own net flux---then
mandating production expansion and 500$^\circ$C conversion at
\$110--330/tCO$_2$e; removal credits instead activate the \$250/t bulk
market at \$25/tCO$_2$e under the calibrated step demand and mobilize most
near-term biomass at \$200/tCO$_2$e (surplus \$3.36~B/yr). The cap's shadow
abatement cost (fixed-layout LP dual,
\$351--599/tCO$_2$e) exceeds observed allowance and credit prices, and the
VM0044 H/C gate reshapes temperature selection. Under the calibrated demand,
policy design---not stringency---sets the scale, technology, and spatial
structure of biochar CDR.
\end{abstract}

\paragraph{Keywords} biochar supply chain; carbon dioxide removal; market
clearing; inherent values; shadow carbon price; baseline-and-credit;
cap-and-trade; tiered carbon tax; VM0044; mixed-integer programming

\section{Introduction}

Biochar, the carbon-rich solid produced by pyrolyzing biomass under oxygen-limited
conditions, is one of the most scalable engineered carbon dioxide removal (CDR)
pathways available today \cite{woolf2010, lehmann2021biochar}. Applying biochar to
soils simultaneously sequesters photosynthetically fixed carbon on centennial
timescales and delivers agronomic co-benefits, including reduced nitrous oxide
emissions and improved water retention \cite{cayuela2014, smith2016}. Recent
assessments estimate that biochar could remove 0.3--2 Gt CO$_2$ per year globally
by mid-century \cite{woolf2021est}, and the 2023 Billion-Ton Report identifies
agricultural residues and forestry wastes in the United States, more than a
billion dry tonnes per year under mature-market scenarios, as an abundant
feedstock base \cite{bt23}. In parallel, voluntary carbon markets have matured to
the point where biochar-based removal credits trade at \$100--250 per tonne
CO$_2$e under methodologies such as Verra VM0044 and Puro.earth CORC, with
corporate buyers purchasing hundreds of thousands of tonnes annually
\cite{cdrfyi2024, verraVM0044}.

Realizing this potential requires coordinating biomass collection, drying,
pyrolysis, biochar distribution, and soil application across hundreds of
facilities and counties, a classical supply chain network design problem with an
unusually strong coupling between economics and carbon accounting. Unlike
conventional biomass-to-energy systems, a biochar system's dominant value stream
under carbon policy is the removal credit itself, which accrues per tonne of
stable carbon rather than per unit of energy sold. This coupling raises
questions that existing models do not answer: ``How should carbon policies be
formulated inside a supply chain optimization model so that they genuinely
change facility locations, technology choices, and production volumes?'' and
``What is the shadow price of carbon that emerges from such a system, and
how does it compare with observed market prices?''

Three strands of literature frame these questions. First, biochar techno-economic
and life-cycle assessments have established the unit economics of slow pyrolysis
and the permanence accounting of biochar carbon
\cite{roberts2010, woolf2010, nematian2021}; these studies, however, treat a
single plant or a stylized regional average and do not optimize network structure.
Second, biomass and negative-emission supply chain optimization has developed
spatially explicit mixed-integer models for biochar source--sink networks
\cite{tan2016biochar, belmonte2018}, BECCS chains \cite{negri2021}, and
multi-product waste networks \cite{sampat2019}. The market-clearing framework of
Sampat et al.~\cite{sampat2019} and the spatio-temporal economic analysis of
Tominac et al.~\cite{tominac2022} are particularly relevant: they show that the
dual variables of mass-balance constraints in an optimization model are the
``inherent values'' of products at each location, i.e., equilibrium prices
under perfect coordination. Third, the green supply chain literature has compared
carbon taxes, cap-and-trade, and carbon credit policies in generic production and
inventory networks \cite{benjaafar2013, zakeri2015, chaabane2012, elhedhli2012},
demonstrating that a linear tax shifts the optimal solution only when emissions
intensity varies endogenously across decisions; it is otherwise a constant
shift of the objective.

This paper addresses the gap between these strands: no existing
study (i) embeds carbon policy inside a multi-product, price-responsive
supply chain network for biochar in the market-clearing/dual framework, (ii)
derives the shadow carbon price of the system as the dual of an
emission constraint and benchmarks it against real market prices, and (iii)
compares three canonical policy architectures (baseline-and-credit,
cap-and-trade, and a tiered tax with removal crediting and allowance
allocation) on identical network, data, and demand structures. We fill this gap
with a case study of Wisconsin, a state whose 14 million acres of farmland, 46\%
forest cover, and dairy-dominated agriculture make it representative of the
Midwestern biomass landscape.

The contributions of this paper are threefold. First, we formulate a
multi-product biochar supply chain network design model, with 72 county nodes,
13 feedstocks, drying plus slow pyrolysis at two temperatures (300 and
500~$^\circ$C), and a three-segment price-responsive biochar demand curve, in
which facility locations are integer decisions and market clearing yields
spatially resolved inherent values from LP duals. Second, we integrate three
carbon policy architectures into this model: (A) a baseline-and-credit mechanism
whose credits scale with throughput; (B) a cap-and-trade scheme in which the
carbon price is the shadow dual of the emission cap, tracing the
system's marginal abatement cost curve; and (C) a tiered carbon tax combined with
Verra VM0044-inspired removal crediting with a molar H/C $\leq$ 0.7
eligibility gate (additionality, leakage, and MRV requirements are outside the
model scope) and
grandfathering versus benchmarking allowance allocation. Third, using DOE
Billion-Ton 2023 (including its USFS FIA-based forestry module) and USDA NASS
2022 Census data, we quantify how each
policy reshapes facility location, technology selection, biochar production
(2.25--2.85 Mt/yr across the policies studied in the near-term scenario),
carbon removal (1.60--1.96 Mt
CO$_2$e/yr), and system surplus (\$0.7--3.4 billion/yr), and we show that net
emission caps looser than the system's baseline flux do not bind while removal
crediting
activates the low-price agricultural segment of demand at credit prices as low as
\$25/tCO$_2$e under the calibrated demand.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section~\ref{sec:model} we present the
mathematical formulation, including the base network model and the three policy
modules, together with three propositions that are proved in the
Supplementary Information: the dual-to-MAC identity of the cap (Prop.~1), the
demand-bound/supply-bound crossover prices that decide whether crediting
upgrades the pyrolysis temperature (Prop.~2), and the dispatch-neutrality
threshold of a linear emission tax in the demand-constrained regime
(Prop.~3). Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results: baseline facility
locations and economics, environmental performance, the market-clearing
inherent values, the policy responses, the quality-differentiated extension,
and one-at-a-time sensitivities. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses policy
implications against real carbon market data, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes; the Wisconsin case-study data and
scenario design are documented in the Supplementary Information.

\subsection{Problem statement}

We consider a regional biochar supply chain operator (a coordinating entity,
per the market-clearing paradigm of \cite{sampat2019,ma2023}) that must decide where to
build biomass drying and pyrolysis facilities, which feedstocks to procure from
which counties, and how to distribute biochar to demand locations, in order to
maximize the system-wide economic surplus (Fig.~\ref{fig:super}). The network
consists of $n \in
\mathcal{N}$ county nodes ($|\mathcal{N}|=72$ for Wisconsin), $f \in \mathcal{F}$
feedstocks ($|\mathcal{F}|=13$: corn stover, soybean straw, wheat straw, oats
straw, barley straw, switchgrass, miscanthus, poplar, willow, logging residues,
small-diameter trees, mill residues, and other forest waste), and a two-stage
technology superstructure: T1 dehydration followed by T2 slow pyrolysis at
either 300 or 500~$^\circ$C. The model jointly determines (i) integer facility
locations and scale tiers, (ii) continuous material flows, and (iii) the
equilibrium quantities served on a price-responsive biochar demand curve. Under
carbon policy, the operator additionally earns removal credits or faces
emission charges, which enter the objective as described in
Section~\ref{sec:policy}.

\subsection{Base network model}

The base model is a multi-product supply chain network design formulation; the
complete statement (with sets, parameters, and the full constraint list) is
given in the SI (Table~S5). Throughout, sets are calligraphic, decision
variables are lowercase, parameters are Greek or barred, and $\delta_{ij}$
denotes the great-circle distance between nodes $i$ and $j$. The operator
maximizes the coordinated-system surplus
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:base}
\begin{align}
\maxi_{z,x,f,\mathrm{dem},\mathrm{sup}}\quad
\Pi & = \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}} \lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}_{d}\,\mathrm{dem}_{d}}_{\text{revenue}}
- \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}} \lambda^{\mathrm{sup}}_{s}\,\mathrm{sup}_{s}}_{\text{farmgate}}
- \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{n,t} \omega_{t}\,(-x_{n,\mathrm{ref}(t),t})}_{\text{OPEX}}
\notag\\
&\quad - \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{(i,j,p)\in\mathcal{A}}(\alpha^{\mathrm{var}}_{p}\delta_{ij}+\alpha^{\mathrm{fix}}_{p})f_{ijp}}_{\text{transport}}
- \underbrace{\mathrm{AF}\textstyle\sum_{n,t,k}\kappa_{t,k}\,z_{ntk}}_{\text{annualized CAPEX}}
\label{eq:base_obj}\\
\st & \quad s_{np} + p_{np} + \textstyle\sum_{j:(j,n,p)\in\mathcal{A}} f_{jnp} =
d_{np} + \textstyle\sum_{j:(n,j,p)\in\mathcal{A}} f_{njp},
\quad (\pi_{np}) \quad n\in\mathcal{N},\ p\in\mathcal{P} \label{eq:base_balance}\\
& \quad x_{n,p,t} = \gamma_{t,p}\,x_{n,\mathrm{ref}(t),t}, \quad n\in\mathcal{N},\ t\in\mathcal{T},\ p\in\mathcal{P} \label{eq:base_yield}\\
& \quad -x_{n,\mathrm{ref}(t),t} \le \textstyle\sum_{k} \bar{q}_{t,k}\,z_{ntk}, \quad n\in\mathcal{N},\ t\in\mathcal{T} \label{eq:base_cap}\\
& \quad z_{ntk}\in\{0,\dots,5\}, \quad 0\le f_{ijp}\le \bar{f}_{ijp}, \quad \mathrm{dem}_d\le\bar{d}_d, \quad \mathrm{sup}_s\le\bar{s}_s \label{eq:base_bounds},
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\mathcal{P}$ collects all products (wet biomass, dry biomass, biochar,
carbon credits), $s_{np}$/$d_{np}$ aggregate the supply and demand bids at node
$n$, and $\gamma_{t,p}$ is the fixed-yield technology transformation.
Constraint \eqref{eq:base_obj} captures demand-side surplus from the bid stack
minus farmgate, operating, transport, and annualized capital costs.
Constraint \eqref{eq:base_balance} is the node mass balance for product $p$;
its dual variable $\pi_{np}$ is the spatial inherent value of product $p$ at
node $n$, the marginal cost of delivering one more unit of $p$ at $n$, which
the market-clearing paradigm interprets as the equilibrium price
\cite{sampat2019,ma2023}. Constraint \eqref{eq:base_yield} enforces the
fixed-yield technology transformations (dehydration water removal, pyrolysis
mass yields, credit co-production), \eqref{eq:base_cap} links throughput to the
integer number of facilities installed at each scale tier $k$, and
\eqref{eq:base_bounds} states integrality and the bid capacities. Transport
radius limits (wet $\leq$150~km, dry $\leq$250~km, biochar $\leq$400~km) are
imposed through $\bar{f}_{ijp}$, consistent with biomass-logistics cost
estimates under which hauling low-density feedstocks becomes prohibitive
beyond roughly 80--160~km \cite{searcy2007}. The annualization factor is
$\mathrm{AF}=0.1175$ ($i{=}10\%$, $n{=}20$~yr); all parameter values and data
sources are tabulated in the SI.

\subsection{Greenhouse gas accounting}

Three GHG components enter the policy modules, all in kg CO$_2$e/yr. Gross
emissions $E^{+}(x)$ comprise process emissions from collection, drying, and
pyrolysis plus transport:
\begin{equation}
E^{+}(x)=\sum_{n,t} e^{\mathrm{proc}}_{t}\bigl(-x_{n,\mathrm{ref}(t),t}\bigr)
+ e^{\mathrm{tr}}\sum_{(i,j,p)\in\mathcal{A}}\delta_{ij}f_{ijp},
\end{equation}
with $e^{\mathrm{proc}}_t$ the technology-specific process intensity per
tonne of the technology's reference product (31--35~kg/t wet, i.e.,
35--51~kg/t dry, for T1, 113--122~kg/t dry for T2 at 500/300$^\circ$C after
the syngas electricity credit)
and $e^{\mathrm{tr}}=0.0902$~kg/t-km. Carbon removal $S(x)$ is defined as a
positive physical quantity: sequestration and N$_2$O suppression per
tonne of biochar applied:
\begin{equation}
S(x)=\sum_{n,\,t\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{pyr}}}
\bigl(r^{\mathrm{seq}}_{t}+r^{\mathrm{N}_2\mathrm{O}}_{t}\bigr)\,x_{n,\mathrm{BC},t},
\qquad
r^{\mathrm{seq}}_{t}=\rho_t\,C_t\,\frac{44}{12}\times 10^{3}\ \text{kg/t},
\end{equation}
where $C_t$ is the biochar carbon content, $\rho_t$ a permanence factor
($\rho_{300}=0.65$, $\rho_{500}=0.80$; VM0044 v1.2 Table~3, drawn from IPCC
2019 Table~4AP.2; the 300$^\circ$C value uses the lowest tabulated band,
350--450$^\circ$C, and the permanence of sub-350$^\circ$C chars may be lower),
and $r^{\mathrm{N}_2\mathrm{O}}_{t}=52$~kg/t from the
meta-analysis of Cayuela et al.~\cite{cayuela2014}. The direct atmospheric flux of the system is therefore
\begin{equation}
N(x)=E^{+}(x)-S(x),
\end{equation}
which is negative (net removal) at the baseline equilibrium. This flux
excludes the decomposition baseline, the emissions avoided by diverting
biomass from natural decay,
\begin{equation}
B(x)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}} b_f \sum_{n}\bigl(-x_{n,f,\mathrm{T1}_f}\bigr),
\qquad b_f = 0.9\,C_f^{\mathrm{dry}}\,\frac{44}{12}\times 10^{3}\ \text{kg/t dry},
\end{equation}
with $b_f \approx 1350$--$1650$~kg/t dry. The decay fraction $0.9$ is a
scenario assumption (immediate, complete decay of uncollected residues, an
upper bound on the avoided-emission credit); a sensitivity halving it to 0.5 is
reported in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}. Throughout the paper, reported
``net GHG'' figures are the direct flux $N(x)$, not abatement relative to
the decay counterfactual; $B(x)$ enters only the credit calculation of
Paradigm~A below.

\subsection{Price-responsive demand and market clearing}

Biochar demand follows a quality-tiered step function (bid stack) at each
county, allocated in proportion to farmland area:
\begin{equation}
\lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}(Q)=\begin{cases}
\$800/\mathrm{t} & Q\le 0.8~\mathrm{Mt}\ \text{(CDR premium: BC500 only in v2b)}\\
\$500/\mathrm{t} & 0.8<Q\le 2.3~\mathrm{Mt}\ \text{(quality ag / industrial media)}\\
\$250/\mathrm{t} & 2.3<Q\le 4.8~\mathrm{Mt}\ \text{(bulk agricultural)}\\
\$30/\mathrm{t}  & \text{sink (construction, filtration)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The three segment prices are anchored to production costs of \$150--400/t
\cite{roberts2010,shabangu2014} plus the value of the co-produced removal
credit (\$100--250/tCO$_2$e \cite{cdrfyi2024,verraVM0044}; $\approx$1.6~tCO$_2$e
per tonne of 500$^\circ$C char, i.e.\ \$160--400/t of biochar) and a quality
premium for durable, H/C-gated product: the \$800/t H tier is durable biochar
sold to national corporate CDR buyers, the \$500/t M tier quality agricultural
and industrial-media grades, and the \$250/t L tier bulk soil application.
This segmentation follows the soil-amendment, industrial-media, and
carbon-removal end uses that structure the biochar market, within the wide
price dispersion reported in current market surveys \cite{ibi2025}. The three soil
segments sum to a finite aggregate capacity of $0.8+1.5+2.5 = 4.8$~Mt/yr,
which is the ceiling on price-responsive biochar sales; the sink is unbounded
(open capacity) and absorbs any residual at the low \$30/t bid. All three
capacities are allocated across counties in proportion to farmland area, a
smooth spatial proxy rather than observed county-level demand; because every
county then carries demand at the same bid, the binding constraint is the
aggregate demand quantity and the equilibrium spatial pattern is
feedstock-driven rather than demand-driven (SI, demand data pack).
Because demand is
price-responsive, the equilibrium quantity is endogenous; in the base model
(v2) biochar is pooled, while the quality-differentiated extension (v2b,
Section~\ref{sec:v2b}) splits biochar into BC300 and BC500 products with the
CDR premium segment restricted to BC500.

\emph{Price concepts.} Four distinct price-like objects appear throughout the
paper, and they are not the same quantity. (i) \emph{Demand bid}
$\lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}_d$: the buyer's willingness-to-pay anchor for one tonne
of biochar in segment $d$, not an equilibrium price. (ii) \emph{Nodal inherent
value} $\pi_{np}$: the dual of the node mass balance, the conditional marginal
value of one more tonne of product $p$ at node $n$ under the fixed facility
layout. (iii) \emph{Shadow carbon price} (conditional fixed-layout marginal
abatement cost): the negative of the cap dual, $-\mu_{\mathrm{CAP}}$, the
marginal opportunity cost of tightening the emission cap under the fixed
layout; it is a coordinating-entity shadow price, not a price from a
decentralized allowance market (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
(iv) \emph{Administered market benchmarks}: observed RGGI/EU ETS allowance and
voluntary removal-credit prices, used only as external comparisons.

\subsection{Carbon policy modules}
\label{sec:policy}

\subsubsection{Paradigm A: baseline-and-credit}

Credits accrue for the net emission reduction relative to the decomposition
baseline, and because the baseline scales with throughput, the credit mechanism
rewards capacity expansion, the property a linear tax lacks:
\begin{equation}
R(x)=B(x)-E^{+}(x)+S(x)\ \ge\ 0,
\qquad \max\ \Pi+\frac{p_c}{1000}R(x),
\qquad p_c\in\{25,50,100,150,200\}\ \mathrm{\$/tCO}_2\mathrm{e}.
\end{equation}
Implementation: carbon credit (CC) is a co-produced product with per-tonne-dry
rates $\gamma_{t,28}=(b_f-e^{\mathrm{proc}}_{t}+(r^{\mathrm{seq}}_t+r^{\mathrm{N}_2\mathrm{O}}_t)\gamma_{t,\mathrm{BC}})/1000$,
sold at the policy price $p_c$. Credits are computed in LCFS style for
every feedstock--temperature pair, without an eligibility gate; the
VM0044 H/C gate is applied only in Paradigm~C2, so the two paradigms differ in
both credit basis (avoided-baseline versus sequestered-carbon) and eligibility
(Fig.~\ref{fig:creditbasis}). Paradigm~A is therefore an idealized
throughput-scaled credit rather than a specific existing methodology; it is
contrasted with the sequestration-only, H/C-gated VM0044 basis of Paradigm~C2.

\subsubsection{Paradigm B: cap-and-trade with shadow carbon price}

B1 imposes a gross-emission cap and sweeps it to trace the system's marginal
abatement cost (MAC) curve $\Pi^{*}(\mathrm{CAP})$:
\begin{equation}
\max\ \Pi \quad \text{s.t.}\quad E^{+}(x)\le \mathrm{CAP},
\qquad \mathrm{MAC}=\frac{\partial \Pi^{*}}{\partial\,\mathrm{CAP}}
\end{equation}
The shadow-price interpretation is formalized as follows.
\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:mac_dual}
For the fixed-layout LP \eqref{eq:base} with an added gross-emission cap
$E^{+}(x)\le\mathrm{CAP}$, let $\mu_{\mathrm{CAP}}$ be the optimal dual
variable of the cap constraint under Gurobi's sign convention for a $\le$
constraint in a maximization. Then
$\mu_{\mathrm{CAP}}=-\partial\Pi^{*}/\partial\,\mathrm{CAP}$: the reported dual
is the negative of the marginal abatement cost, so the shadow carbon price
(conditional on the fixed layout) is $-\mu_{\mathrm{CAP}}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The identity is the standard parametric-LP sensitivity theorem applied to the
cap's right-hand side; the full argument is in the SI (Section~A.9).
\end{proof}
The proposition is verified numerically: the reported dual matches the
finite-difference MAC to numerical tolerance ($-374$ vs.\ \$351--391\$/t over
the first binding intervals in the near-term scenario). We benchmark the
resulting MAC against 2024--2025 RGGI
(\$17--25/t) and EU ETS (\texteuro 50--90/t, $\approx$\$55--100/t) allowance
prices and the
voluntary removal-credit band (\$100--250/t, VM0044/Puro). B2 replaces the
constraint by a net-flux cap
$E^{+}(x)-S(x)\le\mathrm{CAP}^{\mathrm{net}}$. At every tested cap
($\mathrm{CAP}^{\mathrm{net}}\ge-1{,}000$~kt, all looser than the baseline
flux of $-1{,}595$~kt) the constraint does not bind; a frontier sweep that
tightens the cap below the baseline flux (free-facility MIP, SI Fig.~S2)
locates the first binding point and traces the response, which is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsubsection{Which resource binds: demand-bound versus supply-bound regimes}

Whether a policy instrument changes the technology mix is decided by
which resource binds. Define, for each feedstock $f$, the dry-biomass
mass yields of the 300$^\circ$C and 500$^\circ$C pyrolysis routes
$\gamma^3_f$ and $\gamma^5_f$ ($\gamma^3_f>\gamma^5_f$), the credit rates
$\rho^3_f, \rho^5_f$ (tCC/t dry, zero for ineligible 300$^\circ$C chars under
VM0044), the per-tonne dry cost $\omega$ of the full chain (farmgate, OPEX,
transport, annualized CAPEX), and a biochar market price $P$. The two
candidate ``regimes'' correspond to which constraint is active in the
fixed-facility LP:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Demand-bound} (feedstock abundant, e.g.\ mature-market): the
supply of biochar is limited by demand, so the relevant objective margin is
per tonne of biochar served. The 500$^\circ$C margin premium per
served tonne is
\begin{equation}
\Delta^{\mathrm{dem}}_{f}(p_c)=
\Bigl(\tfrac{\rho^5_f}{\gamma^5_f}-\tfrac{\rho^3_f}{\gamma^3_f}\Bigr)p_c
-\omega\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\gamma^5_f}-\tfrac{1}{\gamma^3_f}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
independent of the market price $P$: a lower yield multiplies credit
revenue per tonne served.
\item \textbf{Supply-bound} (feedstock scarce, e.g.\ near-term): the supply of
biochar is limited by feedstock, so the relevant margin is per tonne of dry
biomass processed. The 500$^\circ$C premium per dry tonne is
\begin{equation}
\Delta^{\mathrm{sup}}_{f}(p_c)=
\bigl(\gamma^3_f-\gamma^5_f\bigr)P
-\bigl(\rho^5_f-\rho^3_f\bigr)p_c,
\end{equation}
independent of the chain cost $\omega$: the yield loss is now a revenue loss
($P$ scales it) and the credit-rate gain is compared per dry tonne.
\end{itemize}
\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:regime}
For a fixed feedstock $f$ and biochar market price $P$:
(i) in the demand-bound regime, the 500$^\circ$C route becomes optimal at
the unique crossing price
\begin{equation}
\bar{p}^{\mathrm{dem}}_f = \frac{\omega\,(1/\gamma^5_f-1/\gamma^3_f)}
{\rho^5_f/\gamma^5_f-\rho^3_f/\gamma^3_f},
\end{equation}
which does not depend on $P$ and is strictly positive whenever
$\rho^5_f/\gamma^5_f>\rho^3_f/\gamma^3_f$; and
(ii) in the supply-bound regime, the 500$^\circ$C route becomes optimal at
\begin{equation}
\bar{p}^{\mathrm{sup}}_f = \frac{(\gamma^3_f-\gamma^5_f)\,P}
{\rho^5_f-\rho^3_f},
\end{equation}
which is increasing in $P$ and bounded by the demand-segment price.
Moreover, for the LCFS-style baseline-and-credit rates of Paradigm~A
(Table~S2: feedstock averages $\bar{\gamma}^{3}=0.352$,
$\bar{\gamma}^{5}=0.278$ t BC/t dry, $\bar{\rho}^{3}=1.771$,
$\bar{\rho}^{5}=1.849$ tCC/t dry (the per-dry-tonne rates, whose
$\approx 0.077$ tCC/t dry premium is the credit-rate advantage of
500$^\circ$C)) and the pooled chain cost $\omega\approx\$130$/t dry,
$\bar{p}^{\mathrm{dem}}\approx\$61$/tCO$_2$e for every demand segment and
$\bar{p}^{\mathrm{sup}}\approx\$239$/tCO$_2$e at $P=\$250$/t.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Equating the per-tonne margins of the two routes in each regime and solving
for $p_c$ yields the stated crossing prices; the full argument is in the SI
(Section~A.10).
\end{proof} the policy--resource interaction observed in
Section~\ref{sec:results}: baseline-and-credit never
upgrades the technology mix in the near-term (feedstock-scarce) scenario up to
$p_c\approx\$239$/t, whereas the mature-market (demand-bound) scenario
switches at $p_c\approx\$61$/t for every demand segment; the quality
tier of the premium segment ($\$800$~t$^{-1}$, BC500-only) is unaffected,
since it is already served by 500$^\circ$C in both regimes. The proposition
also explains why Paradigm~C2's VM0044 gate is so much more powerful than a
flat credit price: the gate sets $\rho^3_f=0$, maximizing the numerator in
$\bar{p}^{\mathrm{sup}}_f$, i.e.\ it prices durability rather than tonnes.

\subsubsection{Paradigm C: tiered tax + VM0044 eligibility + allowance allocation}

C1 taxes gross emissions on a convex piecewise-linear schedule with tiers
calibrated inside the feasible emission range ($T_1=0.4E^{+}_0$, $T_2=0.7E^{+}_0$;
rates \$25/50/100 and \$50/100/200 per tCO$_2$e); the flat rate set
(25/25/25) isolates the linear-tax case. To rule out the possibility that the
fixed-layout approximation drives the tax results, C1 is also re-solved as a
free-facility MIP (C1-free). C2 adds removal crediting operationalized through
the molar H/C~$\le$~0.7 eligibility gate as an H/C-gated VM0044-inspired
proxy:
only biochar with molar H/C~$\le$~0.7 generates credits. Measured 300$^\circ$C
chars are torrefaction-like with H/C $\approx$0.9--1.4
\cite{rafiq2016,ippolito2020}, so no 300$^\circ$C biochar qualifies and
its credit rate is zero for every feedstock; 500$^\circ$C chars have H/C
$\approx$0.40--0.52 \cite{rafiq2016,enders2012,ippolito2020} and qualify for
all feedstocks, turning carbon crediting into a technology-selection
instrument. Other VM0044 requirements (additionality
demonstration, leakage accounting, monitoring and verification) are outside the
model scope and their transaction costs are not modeled. C3 compares allowance
allocation rules: grandfathering ($A=E^{+}_0$, fixed) versus benchmarking
($A=\beta q$ with $\beta=E^{+}_0/q_0$), with allowance trades priced at
$p_{\mathrm{allow}}\in\{30,80\}$~\$/tCO$_2$e.

\subsubsection{Why a linear emission tax does not change a demand-constrained system}

The observed null response of the tiered tax on dispatch (Section~\ref{sec:policyC})
is not a modeling artifact but a structural property of the demand-constrained
regime. Formally:
\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:taxinv}
Let $x^{*}$ be an optimal solution of the fixed-layout LP \eqref{eq:base} and
suppose the biochar mass-balance duals (inherent values) satisfy
$\pi^{\mathrm{BC}}_{n} \le \lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}_{d(n)}$ for every served
demand bid $d(n)$ and $\pi^{\mathrm{BC}}_{n} \ge
\lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}_{d}$ for every unserved bid, both with strict
inequalities (the marginal served bid earns a strictly positive margin and
the next, unserved, bid is strictly unprofitable; this is the demand-constrained
regime with a strict gap at the margin). Then for any tax rate
$\tau \in [0, \tau_{\max})$ with
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\max} =
\min_{q\in\mathcal{Q}(x^{*})}
\frac{\lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}_{d(q)}-\pi^{\mathrm{BC}}_{n(q)}}{e^{+}_q},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Q}(x^{*})$ is the set of biochar supply paths that are
marginally profitable at $x^{*}$
($\lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}_{d(q)}>\pi^{\mathrm{BC}}_{n(q)}$) and $e^{+}_q$ is
the gross-emission rate of path $q$ (kg CO$_2$e per tonne of biochar), the
same $x^{*}$ remains optimal in the taxed problem
$\max\ \Pi(x)-\tau\,E^{+}(x)$; the tax reduces only surplus,
$\Pi_{\tau}(x^{*})=\Pi(x^{*})-\tau\,E^{+}(x^{*})$, and leaves the material
flows, technology mix, and equilibrium quantities unchanged.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The tax perturbs only the objective, so the optimal basis is unchanged while
the reduced-cost signs of the active margin paths persist; the full argument is
in the SI (Section~A.11).
\end{proof}
Intuitively, the tax rescales the objective by a constant per-unit-of-activity
penalty that is uniform across the supply paths whose activity is fixed by the
marginal demand bid; as long as the (gross) emission intensity of those paths
is bounded relative to their surplus, the pre-tax ordering of the bid stack is
preserved. In our data, the marginal price gap is
$\lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}-\pi^{\mathrm{BC}} \approx \$155$/t
biochar (the \$500/t M bid minus the largest served-node inherent value,
\$344.85/t). Evaluating \eqref{eq:taumax} with the route-specific marginal
intensities of the LP basis (SI Section~A.11 reports every served demand row
individually), the binding rows are the M-segment rows served by the
corn-stover 300$^\circ$C chain with $e^{+}_q\approx0.48$~t CO$_2$e/t biochar,
so $\tau_{\max}\approx\$321$/t CO$_2$e; the H-segment rows are far looser
($\approx$\$1{,}100/t). The route-specific threshold exceeds the top tested
tier of
\$200/t; the tax's effect within the tested range is confined to surplus
reallocation. The neutrality itself is a structural property of any
demand-constrained LP; the contribution here is the quantified boundary
($\tau_{\max}\approx\$321$/t for this system) and the comparison it enables
with observed carbon prices.

\subsection{Quality-differentiated extension (v2b)}
\label{sec:v2b}

The base model pools biochar into one product. The extension splits it into
BC300 (product 27, from 300$^\circ$C pyrolysis, torrefaction-like H/C
$\approx$0.9--1.4, credit-ineligible) and BC500
(product 28, from 500$^\circ$C, H/C~$\approx$~0.40--0.52, credit-eligible), with
the CDR premium demand
segment ($\$800/t$) restricted to BC500. Yields follow the V9 technology table,
cross-checked against \cite{roberts2010} and \cite{rafiq2016}:
$\gamma^{\mathrm{BC300}}=0.34$--$0.38$ and $\gamma^{\mathrm{BC500}}=0.29$--$0.32$,
with carbon contents 41--50\% and 50--61\%, respectively (the upper 500$^\circ$C
carbon contents are conservative relative to the 62--65\% published range,
Table~S2). This makes the
pyrolysis temperature a genuine economic decision: 500$^\circ$C sacrifices
yield to access the premium market and higher removal credits.

\subsection{Solution strategy}

Facility-location decisions are integer, so Steps 1 (MIP) and the policy sweeps
use Gurobi 13 with 0.5--1\% optimality gap targets, 600--3{,}600~s time limits, and
feasibility-focused branching (MIPFocus=1) with 60~s of zero-objective
heuristics; successive policy prices are warm-started from the previous
facility layout. All dual-based analyses (inherent values, GHG shadow prices, shadow
carbon prices) are performed on LPs with the facility layout fixed
and solved with dual simplex, so that reported duals are vertex
shadow prices; where degeneracy is detected we report the dual range instead
of a point value (SI Section~A.9), and interior-point (barrier) duals of the
cap constraint are not
vertex shadow prices on this LP and are not used. The GHG LP (Step 3) pins demand quantities to the
Step-2 equilibrium, so its duals are marginal GHG values at the market
equilibrium rather than at the GHG-minimal dispatch. The near-term scenario
(6.97~Mt dry biomass) serves as the baseline and the mature-market medium
scenario (13.53~Mt dry) as sensitivity. Full reproducibility scripts and data
are provided in the SI.

\begin{assumption}
\label{ass:duality}
For the fixed-layout LPs, strong duality holds: the reported primal and dual
objective values coincide. Dual solutions need not be unique at degenerate
vertices, so we do not assume uniqueness; throughout, the reported shadow
prices are verified against finite-difference perturbations, and dual ranges
(SI Section~A.9) are reported wherever degeneracy is detected.
\end{assumption}

\subsection{Baseline facility locations and economics}

Without carbon policy, the optimal network serves the two
highest-value demand segments in full and nothing else (Table~\ref{tab:s0};
Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline}). The equilibrium
quantity is 2.30~Mt of biochar per year: the H segment
(\$800/t, 0.80~Mt) and M segment (\$500/t, 1.50~Mt) are fully served, while the

\subsection{Environmental performance}

The direct atmospheric flux $N(x)=E^{+}(x)-S(x)$ is $-1.60$~Mt CO$_2$e/yr
(process $+979$~kt, transport $+24$~kt, carbon removal $2{,}598$~kt) in
near-term and $-1.65$~Mt (process $+967$~kt, transport $+16$~kt, removal
$2{,}631$~kt) under mature-market supply; it excludes
the decomposition baseline $B(x)\approx 1.35$--$1.65$~t CO$_2$e/t dry, which
enters only the Paradigm~A credit calculation (Section 2.3). Process emissions
dominate the gross flux because drying and pyrolysis are energy-intensive per
delivered tonne, while the sequestration term scales directly with biochar
output; the system is therefore net-negative in both scenarios without any
policy intervention, and its environmental profile is a co-product of the
economic equilibrium rather than a separate objective.

\subsection{Value of biochar and biomass: market-clearing inherent values}

The duals of the node mass balances in the fixed-layout LP
(Fig.~\ref{fig:iv}) reveal the spatial
inherent values of biochar and biomass. In the near-term scenario the inherent
value of biochar ranges \$305--345/t (mean \$324/t), i.e., the marginal value of
an additional tonne of biochar available at a county equals its marginal
production, delivery, and capacity-scarcity cost at the fixed baseline
layout; under mature-market supply this falls to \$260--288/t
(mean \$275/t) because abundant low-cost feedstocks (miscanthus, energy crops)
shrink the scarcity rent. Wet biomass inherent values are far below farmgate
prices (\$0--77/t, mean \$9/t in near-term, Fig.~\ref{fig:iv}), confirming
that the system is
demand-constrained: additional biomass supply is nearly worthless without
additional biochar demand. Following the market-clearing paradigm, these duals
are interpreted as equilibrium prices under a coordinating entity; they are
marginal values conditional on the baseline facility layout, not prices
from a decentralized Nash market (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). This
demand-constrained structure is exactly the regime in which a linear carbon tax
fails to move the optimum (cf.~\cite{benjaafar2013}).

\subsection{Paradigm A: baseline-and-credit}
\label{sec:policyA}

Credits that scale with throughput transform the system (Fig.~\ref{fig:policy}).
At $p_c=\$25$/tCO$_2$e the L segment activates under the calibrated step
demand (0.42~Mt served) and production
rises to 2.72~Mt; at $p_c=\$200$/tCO$_2$e the system saturates its feedstock
potential (2.85~Mt, i.e., essentially 100\% of near-term biomass) and surplus
reaches \$3.36~B/yr. In the
mature-market scenario the response is far larger: 4.79~Mt at $p_c=\$25$ and
$\approx$4.80~Mt at $p_c\ge\$50$, with the L segment fully served from
$p_c=\$25$ and production then pinned at the feedstock ceiling; surplus
reaches \$5.87~B/yr at $p_c=\$200$.
All sweep points solve at a 0.5\% gap target with 600~s time limits (the
$p_c=25/50$ points re-solved with 1{,}800~s limits, improving the
incumbents); reported
gaps are 0.95--1.9\% (near-term) and 0.5--3.2\% (mature-market, SI
Table~S3), and TIME\_LIMIT points are feasible incumbents rather than proven
optima, so their production and surplus figures are lower bounds on
the true policy response. A dedicated re-solve of the mature
$p_c=\$100$ point at a 0.1\% gap target (3{,}600~s, archived as
\texttt{policy\_A\_recert2\_v2.csv}) raised its 500$^\circ$C share from
1.75 to 2.81~Mt at a 1.9\% gap, matching the $p_c=\$150$--\$200 points
(2.81~Mt at 1.0--1.7\% gaps).
Whether credits change the system's technology mix is decided by
which resource binds, not by the credit price alone (Fig.~\ref{fig:creditbasis};
Proposition~\ref{prop:regime} formalizes the crossover prices in the
demand-bound and supply-bound regimes).
In the feedstock-scarce near-term scenario the 300$^\circ$C chain remains
optimal for the bulk (L) segment up to $p_c\approx\$239$/t, because its
yield advantage (0.352 versus 0.278~t/t, feedstock averages) outweighs the modest credit-rate premium
of 500$^\circ$C biochar ($\approx$0.077~tCC/t dry); the binding resource is dry
feedstock, so per-dry-tonne margins rule. In the feedstock-abundant
mature-market scenario the binding resource is biochar demand, and
500$^\circ$C's lower yield multiplies credit revenue per tonne of biochar
served: 500$^\circ$C overtakes 300$^\circ$C at $p_c\approx\$61$/t for every
demand segment, and the sweep routes $\approx$2.8~Mt of the 4.8~Mt through
500$^\circ$C at $p_c=\$100$--\$200. Baseline-and-credit therefore scales the system
in both regimes, but induces a quality upgrade only where feedstock is
abundant, a policy--resource interaction that a single-regime analysis
cannot reveal.
These credits are LCFS-style baseline-and-credit, granted to every
feedstock--temperature pair without an eligibility gate; the VM0044 H/C
gate is applied only in Paradigm~C2 (Section~\ref{sec:policyC}), so Paradigms~A
and C differ in credit basis as well as eligibility
(Fig.~\ref{fig:creditbasis}). The direct atmospheric flux $N(x)=E^{+}(x)-S(x)$
deepens from $-1.60$ to $-1.96$~Mt CO$_2$e/yr as credit prices rise, i.e., net
removal grows with mobilization.

\subsection{Paradigm B: cap-and-trade and the shadow carbon price}

The gross-emission cap sweep traces the system's marginal abatement cost curve
(Fig.~\ref{fig:policy}). Tightening the cap from the baseline 1{,}003~kt to
501~kt reduces surplus from \$708~M to \$451~M/yr and biochar from 2.30 to
1.19~Mt (mature-market: 983$\rightarrow$492~kt, \$736$\rightarrow$\$458~M).
The finite-difference marginal abatement cost is
$\approx$ \$351--391/t CO$_2$e over the first binding intervals and rises to
$\approx$ \$599/t as the cap tightens (mature-market $\approx$ \$468--484
$\rightarrow$ \$603/t), above both 2024--2025 EU ETS allowance prices
(\texteuro 50--90/t, $\approx$\$55--100/t) and the voluntary removal-credit
band (\$100--250/t).
The cap's vertex dual reproduces these values to numerical tolerance: with
Gurobi's sign
convention the reported dual is the negative of the marginal value, i.e.,
$-\$374$/t at the first binding cap (near-term), inside the
\$351--391/t finite-difference band of the first binding intervals, validating the
shadow-price interpretation. Gross emissions are
intrinsic to drying and pyrolysis, so abating them means forgoing biochar
sales, whereas carbon removal is a co-benefit of production; pricing
gross emissions is therefore an expensive lever for this system. The net-flux cap
experiment completes the picture: for $\mathrm{CAP}^{\mathrm{net}}\in[-1{,}000, +1{,}000]$~kt the
constraint $E^{+}(x)-S(x)\le\mathrm{CAP}^{\mathrm{net}}$
does not bind ($\pi=0$) and surplus stays at \$708~M/yr, while a cap of
$-2{,}000$~kt is infeasible under the incumbent dispatch. Every tested cap is
looser than the baseline flux of $-1{,}595$~kt, so this experiment only
establishes that \emph{loose} net caps provide no incentive. A frontier sweep
that tightens the cap below the baseline flux shows where the constraint first
binds and how the system responds (SI Fig.~S2): from $-1{,}600$ to
$-1{,}800$~kt the system deepens its net flux by expanding production into the
unprofitable L segment (2.30$\rightarrow$2.61~Mt, all 300$^\circ$C) at a
finite-difference marginal cost of $\approx$\$110/tCO$_2$e; below
$-1{,}900$~kt further deepening requires converting production to 500$^\circ$C
pyrolysis (0.13$\rightarrow$1.12~Mt over $-1{,}900$ to $-2{,}200$~kt) at
$\approx$\$150--330/tCO$_2$e (finite-difference estimates between
TIME\_LIMIT incumbents), and the minimum feasible net flux is
$-2{,}602$~kt (all-500$^\circ$C, 2.26~Mt, solved to optimality). A net cap is
therefore not blind to negative emissions: it binds once tightened below the
system's own flux, first as an expansion mandate and then as a durability
mandate, with a shadow cost comparable to gross-emission abatement.

\subsection{Paradigm C: tiered tax, VM0044 eligibility, and allocation}
\label{sec:policyC}

The tiered tax (C1) reduces surplus (708 to 653~M\$/yr at rates 25/50/100) but
does not alter the fixed-facility dispatch, consistent with the
demand-constrained regime (Proposition~\ref{prop:taxinv} establishes that a
linear tax cannot change the dispatch when demand is marginally binding).
To rule out the possibility that this null response
is an artifact of the fixed layout, we re-solve C1 as a free-facility MIP
(C1-free): the optimizer finds a layout equivalent to the baseline within the
MIP gap at every rate set tested (Table~\ref{tab:c1free}; 75--80 T1 and
38--40 T2 units versus 78 and 38 at baseline, with gross emissions within
1\% of the baseline dispatch). The free-facility surplus (653.3~M\$/yr)
matches the fixed-layout LP (653.2~M\$/yr) to within the MIP gap, and the same
holds for the steeper (598.3 vs 598.0~M\$/yr) and linear (683.2 vs
683.2~M\$/yr) schedules, confirming that the tax's ineffectiveness
is economic (demand, not emissions, binds) rather than a modeling
artifact. Adding VM0044 removal crediting with free facilities (C2) changes
technology selection directly, because the eligibility gate makes
no 300$^\circ$C biochar credit-eligible: measured 300$^\circ$C chars are
torrefaction-like (H/C $\approx$0.9--1.4), while 500$^\circ$C chars
(H/C $\approx$0.40--0.52) qualify for every feedstock. At $p_c=\$50$ the
near-term system is unchanged (2.30~Mt, all 300$^\circ$C), but the
mature-market system already routes 0.50~Mt to 500$^\circ$C; at
$p_c=\$100$ the incumbent solutions route all production through 500$^\circ$C
in both scenarios: 2.22~Mt
(near-term) and 2.71~Mt (mature-market). At
$p_c=\$200$ production is 2.25~Mt (near-term, surplus \$1{,}141~M/yr, 3.64~Mt
of removal credits) and 4.25~Mt (mature-market, surplus \$1{,}501~M/yr,
7.03~Mt credits), entirely 500$^\circ$C (Fig.~\ref{fig:policy}). Two features
of this result are notable. First, the gate does not subsidize
low-durability biochar: every credited tonne satisfies H/C$\le$0.7, and the
credits purchase durability rather than tonnes. The near-term system's
2.25~Mt falls below the 2.30~Mt H+M demand target because 500$^\circ$C
yields less char per dry tonne, and the mature-market 4.25~Mt remains below
the 4.80~Mt achieved under ungated baseline-and-credit crediting
(Section~\ref{sec:policyA}). Second, full conversion raises gross emissions
(E$^{+}$ grows from 1{,}003 to 1{,}204~kt near-term and from 983 to
2{,}239~kt mature-market) because each delivered biochar tonne now requires
more drying and pyrolysis throughput, exactly the interaction a tiered
emission tax is designed to price. A decomposition experiment isolates the
gate's contribution: with the eligibility gate removed (every char
credit-eligible), the \$200/t near-term system keeps 99\% of production on
300$^\circ$C (2.75 of 2.77~Mt), i.e., the technology conversion is driven by
the eligibility gate rather than by the sequestration credit basis alone (SI
Table~S8). The
allowance-allocation comparison (C3) shows no difference between grandfathering
and benchmarking under fixed facilities: with the layout fixed, gross

\subsection{Quality-differentiated biochar (v2b)}

Splitting biochar into BC300 and BC500 with the \$800/t CDR premium segment
restricted to BC500 changes the baseline itself: the optimal no-policy network
builds 14~T2-500$^\circ$C facilities and produces 0.80~Mt of BC500, the full

(Fig.~\ref{fig:creditbasis}, Table~\ref{tab:v2b}); the
premium market alone justifies high-temperature pyrolysis. The v2b no-policy
surplus (\$637~M/yr near-term, \$675~M/yr mature-market) is lower than the
pooled-baseline surplus
(\$708~M/yr) because serving the premium segment with BC500 incurs the higher
energy cost and lower yield of 500$^\circ$C pyrolysis; the two models therefore
define different counterfactuals, and cross-model comparisons should focus on
production and technology mix rather than surplus levels. The response to
baseline-and-credit prices follows the two-regime logic of the pooled model.
In the feedstock-scarce near-term scenario, the bulk expansion is served
entirely by BC300 (1.50$\rightarrow$1.84~Mt), and BC500 stays pinned at the
0.80~Mt premium segment, because per-dry-tonne margins keep 300$^\circ$C
optimal for the bulk segment up to $p_c\approx\$239$/t
(Fig.~\ref{fig:creditbasis}). In the
feedstock-abundant mature-market scenario the demand-bound regime takes over:
BC500 rises from 0.80 to 2.77--2.83~Mt at $p_c=\$100$--\$200, roughly 60\% of
the 4.8~Mt total, as 500$^\circ$C's lower yield multiplies credit revenue per
tonne of biochar served above $p_c\approx\$61$/t. Technology selection is
therefore jointly driven by the credit price and the resource regime,
a policy--resource interaction that a pooled-biochar model without quality
differentiation cannot fully reveal, since it also cannot see the
premium-segment floor on BC500.

\subsection{One-at-a-time sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

The baseline solution is far more sensitive to demand-side than to supply-side
parameters (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}). Demand price $\pm$20\% moves the system
surplus by $\pm$39\% (from \$430~M to \$986~M/yr) while leaving the quantity
at the demand-constrained equilibrium; a 20\% reduction in demand capacity
reduces surplus by 21\% and quantity by 20\%. In contrast, farmgate prices
$\pm$20\% change surplus by only $\pm$9\% and transport costs $\pm$20\% by about
$\pm$1\%; the sink price has no effect because the sink is not activated.

A
40\% reduction in feedstock availability (a proxy for realistic
field-collection losses) reduces production to 1.48~Mt and surplus by 29\% in
the near-term scenario (from 708.3 to 505.9~M\$/yr), but only by 4\% in the
mature-market scenario: the
system is demand-constrained at the aggregate level, but its preferred
feedstock mix (near-term corn stover) is supply-tight, so availability shocks
matter through the composition of supply even when total demand binds.
These one-at-a-time perturbations hold the baseline facility layout
fixed, so they measure dispatch-level responses; layout-level responses are
captured separately by the free-facility policy sweeps (Paradigms A, C1-free,
and C2). The asymmetry confirms that demand-side instruments (adoption support,
removal crediting) are the effective levers for expanding biochar CDR in
Wisconsin, while feedstock-availability risk concentrates in the corn-stover
supply chain. Two policy-relevant parameter sensitivities complete the
picture. First, the decomposition-baseline assumption (0.9 decay fraction)
enters only Paradigm~A's credit rates: halving it to 0.5 lowers the
\$200/t surplus from \$3.36 to \$2.33~B/yr ($-31$\%) in the near-term and from
\$5.87 to \$3.81~B/yr ($-35$\%) mature-market, while mobilized production stays
near the feedstock potential; the qualitative response and the paradigm
ordering are robust, but the surplus level scales roughly one-for-one with the
baseline assumption and A's magnitude advantage over the gated VM0044 credit
narrows as the decomposition fraction falls. A joint carbon-parameter grid
(decomposition fraction $\{0.5,0.7,0.9\}\times$ permanence multipliers
$\{0.8,1.0,1.2\}$, nine free-facility MIP re-solves at $p_c=\$200$/t, gaps
1.2--1.7\%) shows the two parameters act nearly additively: halving the
decomposition fraction lowers the \$200/t surplus by 31--34\% at every
permanence level, a $\pm$20\% permanence perturbation moves the surplus by
only $\mp$3--5\%, and the near-term technology mix remains essentially
all-300$^\circ$C (at most 0.22~Mt of 500$^\circ$C) in every cell (SI,
carbon-parameter scenarios). Second,
the premium-segment capacity directly sets the scale of 500$^\circ$C
deployment: halving H to 0.4~Mt halves BC500 to 0.40~Mt (total production
1.90~Mt), and enlarging H by 50\% (1.2~Mt) raises BC500 to 1.20~Mt and total
production to 2.52~Mt near-term (2.70~Mt mature); the premium market, not
production cost, is the binding driver of high-temperature pyrolysis.

\section{Policy Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why a linear carbon tax fails and what works instead}

The baseline system is demand-constrained: at the equilibrium, the binding
constraints are the biochar demand segments, not biomass availability. In this
regime a linear tax $\tau E^{+}(x)$ on gross emissions is a near-constant shift
of the objective (gross emissions change little across feasible dispatches), so
the primal optimum does not move, precisely the failure mode characterized by
\cite{benjaafar2013}. Proposition~\ref{prop:taxinv} states the boundary
condition precisely: the tax is dispatch-neutral up to a threshold whose
denominator is the marginal price gap divided by the path emission intensity,
which we compute at $\approx$\$321/t CO$_2$e with route-specific marginal
intensities of the LP basis (SI Section~A.11), above every tested rate
(\$25--200/t) but close enough that substantially higher taxes could bite. The
free-facility re-solve (C1-free, Section~\ref{sec:policyC})
confirms that this null response is economic rather than an artifact of the
fixed layout. The claim's boundary condition is equally important: it holds
because demand binds; in a supply-constrained variant of the system
(e.g., feedstock scarcity binding before demand) a tax would bite, and the
statement should not be read as tax-neutrality of biochar systems in general.
Our formulation shows that policy instruments which
scale with throughput (baseline-and-credit) or which act as
constraints (gross emission caps) do change the solution: credits
activate the \$250/t bulk-agricultural demand segment at $p_c=\$25$/tCO$_2$e
under the calibrated step demand and
mobilize essentially all near-term biomass at \$200/tCO$_2$e, while caps reshape
production along the marginal abatement cost curve. The net-flux cap
experiment completes the picture: every tested cap
($\mathrm{CAP}^{\mathrm{net}}\ge-1{,}000$~kt) is looser than the baseline flux
of $-1{,}595$~kt and therefore does not bind; the frontier sweep of SI
Fig.~S2 shows that caps tightened below the baseline flux bind in two
stages---expansion into the L segment at $\approx$\$110/tCO$_2$e, then
500$^\circ$C conversion at $\approx$\$150--330/tCO$_2$e. Policy
design for negative-emission supply chains must therefore
explicitly value removal, not merely penalize emissions.

\subsection{Shadow carbon prices versus real markets}

The conditional fixed-layout shadow marginal abatement cost traced by the
gross-emission cap sweep
is $\approx$ \$351--391/t CO$_2$e over the first binding intervals and rises to
$\approx$ \$599/t as the cap tightens; the cap's vertex dual reproduces it
to numerical tolerance (the reported value is the negative of the marginal
value under
Gurobi's sign convention), validating the shadow-price
interpretation on a full supply chain network. This places the Wisconsin
biochar
system's gross-emission abatement above 2024--2025 RGGI allowance prices
(\$17--25/t), EU ETS prices (\texteuro 50--90/t, $\approx$\$55--100/t), and the
voluntary removal
credit band (\$100--250/tCO$_2$e for VM0044/Puro.earth credits). Two
implications follow. First, at any observed carbon price, a cap-and-trade
program alone would induce little gross abatement in this system, because
gross emissions are intrinsic to drying and pyrolysis and abating them means
forgoing biochar sales; reaching the \$800/t-market equilibrium required no
policy at all, but expanding to the bulk agricultural segment requires credit
prices above roughly \$25/tCO$_2$e under the calibrated demand, well within
observed voluntary-market
prices. Second, the comparison of the system's shadow abatement cost with
administered market prices shows that removal crediting, not
gross-emission pricing, is the mechanism that can profitably expand biochar
CDR, a quantitative argument for designing negative-emission policies around
the removal, rather than around the gross emissions, of such systems.

\subsection{Correcting a common policy misattribution: 45Q}

We note explicitly that the U.S.~45Q tax credit, frequently cited in biochar
investment documents, does not apply to biochar. 45Q credits captured
CO$_2$ streams (point-source CCS and DAC); biochar carbon is sequestration, not
capture, and falls outside the statute. Biochar removal credits are instead
issued under voluntary methodologies (Verra VM0044, which requires molar
H/C~$\le$~0.7 and centennial durability, and Puro.earth CORC); VM0044 credits
were endorsed by the ICVCM as high-integrity in 2025 \cite{icvcm2025}. The
policy landscape is, however, in motion: legislation under consideration in the
119th Congress (e.g., the Wildfire Reduction and Carbon Removal Act of 2025,
S.~1842) would create a federal biochar carbon-removal incentive and reform
45Q, which could materially change the economics quantified here
\cite{wrca2025}. Our Paradigm C2 operationalizes the VM0044 gate with
literature-consistent H/C values: 300$^\circ$C biochar is torrefaction-like
(H/C $\approx$0.9--1.4) and earns no credits for any feedstock, while
all 500$^\circ$C chars (H/C $\approx$0.40--0.52) qualify. The model responds
by converting the system to 500$^\circ$C pyrolysis as credit prices rise: a
partial switch already at \$50/t in the mature-market scenario, complete at
\$100/t, and full conversion (2.25~Mt near-term, 4.25~Mt
mature-market) at \$200/t; the conversion
buys durability at the cost of quantity: near-term production falls slightly
below the 2.30~Mt H+M demand target because 500$^\circ$C yields less char per
dry tonne. The gate switches technology without ever subsidizing
low-durability biochar, an example of
policy detail directly reshaping technology selection.

\subsection{Technology selection and quality differentiation}

Two mechanisms drive pyrolysis temperature choice, and which one binds is
itself the finding. First, when dry feedstock is scarce (near-term), per-dry-tonne
margins rule: 300$^\circ$C dominates for the bulk (L) segment up to
$p_c\approx\$239$/t, because its yield advantage (0.352 vs 0.278~t/t)
outweighs the modest credit-rate premium of 500$^\circ$C biochar. Second, when
feedstock is abundant (mature-market), the binding resource is biochar demand,
and 500$^\circ$C's lower yield becomes an asset: each tonne of biochar
served requires more dry tonnes, each earning credits, so 500$^\circ$C overtakes
300$^\circ$C at $p_c\approx\$61$/t for every segment; the model switches
$\approx$2.8~Mt of production at \$100--\$200/t (the \$100/t point from a
dedicated 3{,}600~s re-solve at a 1.9\% gap, archived as
\texttt{policy\_A\_recert2\_v2.csv}). Third, quality-differentiated
demand (v2b) adds a structural floor: the \$800/t premium segment is BC500-only,
so the no-policy baseline already builds 0.80~Mt/yr of BC500 capacity, and
varying the premium capacity moves BC500 one-for-one (0.40~Mt at half capacity,
1.20~Mt at 1.5$\times$). The market structure (whether buyers differentiate
biochar by durability) therefore matters as much as the carbon price, and the
resource regime decides which margin arithmetic applies.

\subsection{Implications for Wisconsin and the Midwest}

The analysis yields three region-specific messages; magnitudes are calibrated
to Wisconsin, while the mechanisms should transfer qualitatively across the
Upper Midwest (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). (i) Demand, not feedstock,
is the binding constraint: Wisconsin's near-term biomass could support roughly
double the equilibrium biochar production if the \$250/t bulk market is
activated; policies that subsidize farmer adoption (or that value carbon at
voluntary-market rates) expand the system more effectively than supply-side
measures. (ii) Facility locations concentrate in the corn belt and shift with
supply scenarios: mature-market supply lowers biochar's spatial inherent value
from \$324 to \$275/t, flattening location rents. (iii) The dairy-dominated
waste streams (manure, paper-mill sludge) that characterize Wisconsin are
outside this pyrolysis-focused model but form a natural co-location layer for
anaerobic-digestion integrated bioeconomies, an explicit direction for the
multi-pathway extension.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several assumptions bound the interpretation of these results. (i)
\emph{Market clearing.} The model solves a single coordinated system optimum
of Sampat et al.~\cite{sampat2019}; the dual-based ``equilibrium prices'' are marginal
values under perfect coordination, not outcomes of a decentralized Nash market,
so magnitudes (especially policy responses) should be read as
coordination-consistent benchmarks. (ii) \emph{Technology space.} Only slow
pyrolysis at two temperatures (300/500$^\circ$C) is modeled; intermediate
temperatures and competing conversion routes (gasification, hydrothermal
carbonization) are excluded, so the technology-choice conclusions are discrete
comparisons of two points, not a continuous design response. (iii)
\emph{Transport radii.} Hard distance limits (150/250/400~km) approximate
continuous logistics costs; they follow literature cost benchmarks but truncate
the feasible region. (iv) \emph{Feedstock availability.} BT23 tonnages are
treated as fully collectable; field-level collection losses and residue
retention requirements are not modeled. A $-40\%$ availability sensitivity
costs 29\% of near-term surplus (the preferred corn-stover mix is
supply-tight) but only 4\% in the mature scenario, so the assumption matters
mostly for near-term deployment.
(v) \emph{GHG parameters.} The decomposition fraction (0.9), the permanence
factors (0.65/0.80, now aligned with VM0044 v1.2 Table 3), the
literature-based char H/C values (Table~S2; the eligibility outcome is inert
to $\pm$0.1 H/C and the first 500$^\circ$C flip needs $+0.25$, SI Section on
eligibility sensitivity), and the N$_2$O
meta-analysis mean (52~kg/t) are literature defaults; the decomposition
fraction is flagged for local calibration, and a sensitivity halving it to
0.5 lowers the \$200/t Paradigm~A surplus by 31--35\%
(Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}); the
qualitative response and the paradigm ordering are robust, but the surplus
level is not, and A's magnitude advantage over the gated VM0044 credit narrows
as the decomposition fraction falls. (vi) \emph{VM0044 proxy.} Eligibility is
operationalized through the molar H/C gate only; additionality, leakage, and
monitoring/verification costs are outside the model scope. (vii)
\emph{Demand.} Demand segments are exogenous step functions calibrated to
market evidence rather than estimated elasticities, and their spatial
allocation across counties is proportional to farmland area rather than
observed demand locations. The binding demand constraint is therefore the
aggregate quantity, and the equilibrium facility pattern is feedstock-driven;
a demand-concentration sensitivity (SI, demand data pack) leaves the aggregate
conclusions unchanged. (viii) \emph{Static
structure.} The model is single-year with no seasonal inventory; the
allocation-design result (C3) and facility-path dependencies point to a
multi-period extension. (ix) \emph{Solver gaps.} Policy sweeps are reported at
0.5--4.7\% MIP optimality gaps (Table~S3). The largest-gap points were
re-solved at a 0.1\% target with 3{,}600~s limits and multiple seeds: the
near-term C2 $p_c=\$150$ point improved from a 4.7\% to a 2.5\% gap (surplus
$+1.9\%$), and forward versus reverse sweep orders reproduce every point
within 0.2\%, so the reported structural responses are stable across
warm-start paths; TIME\_LIMIT points remain feasible incumbents. (x) \emph{Scope.} Wisconsin-specific magnitudes do not transfer
verbatim to other Midwest states (feedstock mix, demand, and dairy waste
streams differ); the qualitative policy mechanisms are the transferable
findings.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a multi-product, market-clearing supply chain model for a
Wisconsin biochar CDR system and embedded three carbon policy architectures in
it. We report three findings. First, for this demand-constrained, net-negative
network, policy design matters more than stringency within the tested ranges:
a linear carbon tax and net
emission caps looser than the baseline flux leave the optimum unchanged---
tightened below it, the cap binds first as an expansion mandate and then as a
500$^\circ$C durability mandate at \$110--330/tCO$_2$e---
while throughput-scaled removal credits activate the bulk
biochar market at \$25/tCO$_2$e under the calibrated step demand and mobilize
essentially all near-term biomass
at \$200/tCO$_2$e, increasing system surplus from \$0.71~B to \$3.36~B/yr and
deepening net removal from 1.60 to 1.96~Mt CO$_2$e/yr. Second, the shadow
marginal abatement cost (fixed-layout LP dual) traced by the cap-and-trade
sweep ($\approx$ \$351--391/t CO$_2$e
over the first binding intervals, rising to $\approx$ \$599/t) lies above
EU ETS allowance prices and the voluntary removal-credit band, quantifying how
expensive gross-emission abatement is for a pyrolysis-based system, and why
removal crediting, not gross-emission pricing, is the effective policy lever.
Third, regulatory detail measurably reshapes
technology: the VM0044 H/C~$\le$~0.7 gate excludes all torrefaction-like
300$^\circ$C biochar from crediting and converts the system to 500$^\circ$C
pyrolysis as credit prices rise (2.25~Mt near-term, 4.25~Mt mature-market at
\$200/tCO$_2$e), and
quality-differentiated demand alone sustains 0.8~Mt/yr of 500$^\circ$C biochar
in the no-policy baseline.

For Wisconsin and the Midwest, the practical message is that feedstock is not
the constraint; demand is. Policies that underwrite farmer adoption or
monetize durable carbon at voluntary-market rates expand the system far more
effectively than supply-side measures, and the spatial structure of value
(biochar inherent values of \$275--324/t across scenarios) implies that
location rents are sensitive to how much biomass the market ultimately calls
for. Extensions under development include seasonal inventory and multi-period
investment, the integration of anaerobic-digestion waste streams that
characterize the dairy economy, and tighter optimality certification on
high-performance computing for the free-facility policy sweeps.

\section{Data sources and processing}

\subsection{Biomass availability}

County-level feedstock availability (dry tonnes/yr) comes from the DOE 2023
Billion-Ton Report (BT23) county-level downloads
(\texttt{bioenergykdf.ornl.gov}), agricultural and forestry modules; the BT23
forestry module draws on USFS Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) data, which
is therefore the underlying forestry source cited in the main text. The
baseline uses the \emph{near-term} scenario; the \emph{mature-market medium}
scenario serves as sensitivity. The BT23 corn stover series is constructed
from grain-corn production via a 1:1 stover-to-grain ratio and is therefore
\emph{exclusive of silage corn acreage by construction} (silage is absent from
the POLYSYS crop list; the 2022 Census of Agriculture reports 0.81~M acres of
corn silage versus 3.23~M acres of grain corn harvested (20.1\% of corn
acreage), so erroneously including silage would inflate stover by roughly
25\%; the county-level grain/silage split is archived for a silage-inclusive
sensitivity). The download field \texttt{price\_offered} is the
farmgate bid threshold (2022 USD per dry short ton, ash-inclusive, excluding
transport); the near-term scenario represents approximately 2030 conditions
with residues only and a \$70/dry-ton price ceiling. The tonnages are
theoretical availability: field-level collection losses, residue-retention
requirements, and landowner participation are not modeled (a $-40\%$
availability sensitivity is reported in the main text). Feedstock mapping: corn
stover, miscanthus, switchgrass, willow, and poplar map directly from the
agricultural download; logging residues aggregate the five BT23 logging-residue
classes (hardwood lowland/upland, mixedwood, softwood natural/planted);
small-diameter trees aggregate the four SDT classes; mill residues aggregate
hardwood and softwood processing residues; other forest waste corresponds to
``forest waste human generated.'' Wheat, oats, barley, and soybean straw are
taken from USDA NASS 2022 Census of Agriculture county production data
converted to theoretical residue tonnage (NASS Census residue estimates,
archived in the project workbook sheet \texttt{02\_NASS\_Validation};

for corn stover (0.38/0.30~t/t; 0.45/0.55) are the advisor-confirmed
technology-table values (V9 workbook, sheet 02); per-feedstock 500$^\circ$C
values use the documented scaling
$y_{500}=0.789\,y_{300}$, $C_{500}=1.222\,C_{300}$. Source audit: Roberts et
al.\ \cite{roberts2010} is a 450$^\circ$C slow-pyrolysis LCA and contains no

its char-yield range of 28.8--33.0~wt\% (citing Antal and Gr{\o}nli 2003,
\emph{Ind.\ Eng.\ Chem.\ Res.}\ 42:1619), it matches the Rafiq et al.\ corn-stover
500$^\circ$C yield of 29.2\% \cite{rafiq2016}, and it supports
$\rho_{500}=0.80$ through its 80\% stable-carbon assumption. Woolf et
al.\ \cite{woolf2010} do not tabulate temperature-series yields or H/C
ratios. The H/C values are taken from the temperature-series literature
(Table~S2): 300$^\circ$C chars have H/C $=1.00$--$1.40$ (torrefaction-like;
Rafiq et al.\ \cite{rafiq2016} measure 1.41 for corn stover at 300$^\circ$C,
and the Ippolito et al.\ meta-analysis \cite{ippolito2020} places sub-400$^\circ$C
chars in the ``partially thermochemically altered'' range), so
\emph{no} 300$^\circ$C biochar meets the VM0044 H/C$\le$0.7 gate, and its
credit rate is zero throughout. The 500$^\circ$C chars have H/C
$=0.40$--$0.52$ (Rafiq et al.\ \cite{rafiq2016}: corn stover 0.49, benchmarked
against Enders et al.\ \cite{enders2012}; the Ippolito et al.\ meta-analysis
500--599$^\circ$C band mean is $\approx$0.54), comfortably below the gate, so
all 500$^\circ$C biochar is credit-eligible. A $\pm$0.1 H/C perturbation
changes no eligibility outcome (Section~A.7); the first flip of a 500$^\circ$C
char requires $+0.25$, which is the stress test reported there. The
$C_{500}=0.55$ value is conservative relative to the published 500$^\circ$C
carbon contents of 62.5\% (Ippolito et al.\ \cite{ippolito2020} band mean)
and 64.5\% (Rafiq et al.\ \cite{rafiq2016}), so 500$^\circ$C credit rates are
conservative lower bounds.

\subsection{Demand data pack}

The three-segment biochar demand curve is calibrated to: (i) production-side
costs of \$150--400/t \cite{roberts2010,shabangu2014} plus the removal-credit
value of \$100--250/tCO$_2$e \cite{cdrfyi2024,verraVM0044}, which places the
durable-char tiers above the bulk soil-amendment tier; (ii) the
soil-amendment, industrial-media, and carbon-removal end uses that structure
the biochar market, with wholesale prices in
recent market surveys spanning more than an order of magnitude
\cite{ibi2025}; (iii)
Wisconsin farmland of 14.2~M acres
with application rates of 5--20~t/ha, within the range of field studies
reviewed by Jeffery et al.~\cite{jeffery2011}, and adoption below 100\%, so
that the
4.16~Mt aggregate of the previous model version is treated as an optimistic
peak; and (iv) a non-soil sink valued at \$30/t (construction/filtration media)
with a landfill disposal sensitivity of $-\$45$ to $-\$84$/t. Segment
capacities are allocated across counties in proportion to farmland area; the
CDR-premium segment is interpreted as access to national corporate buyers
through credit markets rather than local Wisconsin demand, and the farmland
proportionality is a spatial proxy (county-specific adoption data are not
available). Because every county carries demand at the same bid, the binding
demand constraint is aggregate and the equilibrium spatial pattern is
feedstock-driven; the concentration sensitivity below tests this directly.
The 0.8~Mt premium capacity is a national-market allocation:
current U.S. biochar purchases reported by CDR.fyi are in the hundreds of
thousands of tonnes per year \cite{cdrfyi2024}, so the segment assumes the
several-fold market growth projected for biochar credits rather than today's
volumes. The decomposition fraction $0.9$ is an upper bound (immediate,
complete decay); a sensitivity to $0.7$/$0.5$ scales the Paradigm~A credit
rates proportionally and is flagged in the Limitations section.

\emph{Demand-geography sensitivity.} The baseline allocates each demand
segment across counties in proportion to farmland area ($w_n \propto
\mathrm{area}_n$). To test whether the equilibrium depends on this spatial
proxy, we re-solve the Step-1 MIP and Step-2 LP with demand concentrated as
$w_n \propto \mathrm{area}_n^{\alpha}$ for $\alpha \in \{3, 10\}$; at
$\alpha=10$ the three largest counties carry 71\% of the H-segment capacity
(versus 8\% at $\alpha=1$). Aggregate production (2.30~Mt/yr), wet-biomass
use (6.91--6.96~Mt/yr), and system surplus (708.2, 701.8, and
689.8~M\$/yr for $\alpha=1,3,10$) change by at most 2.6\%, and 94--95\% of the
counties that host facilities are identical across the three cases (58/61 at
$\alpha=3$, 59/63 at $\alpha=10$): the optimal spatial pattern is
feedstock-driven and robust to demand geography. The biochar inherent values
do shift (mean \$324, \$304, and \$476/t for $\alpha=1,3,10$), because they
are nodal prices: at $\alpha=10$ the concentrated demand makes the \$500/t
M-segment bid bind at the demand counties and the inherent value propagates to
the transport-radius limit (\$500/t minus up to \$32/t of delivery cost),
exactly as the market-clearing interpretation predicts.

\subsection{Eligibility sensitivity: H/C uncertainty and the gate}

The VM0044 gate is applied to the literature-consistent H/C values of
Table~S2. A $\pm$0.1 perturbation around those central values changes
\emph{no} eligibility outcome: every 300$^\circ$C value remains above the gate
($1.00$--$1.40$ minus $0.1$ is still $\ge0.90>0.7$), and every 500$^\circ$C
value remains below it ($0.40$--$0.52$ plus $0.1$ is still
$\le0.62<0.7$). The first flip of a 500$^\circ$C char therefore requires a
$+0.25$ stress, well beyond the published 500$^\circ$C spread of
0.40--0.55, which pushes the agricultural-residue chars (corn stover
$0.49\rightarrow0.74$; soybean/wheat/oats/barley/switchgrass
$\ge0.72$) over the gate while forestry and miscanthus chars
($0.40$--$0.45\rightarrow0.65$--$0.70$) remain eligible. Table~S4 re-solves
Paradigm C2 under that stress. At $p_c=\$50$ nothing changes in either
scenario. At $p_c=\$200$ the canonical full conversion is curtailed: near-term
production falls back to 2.30~Mt of which only 0.31~Mt is 500$^\circ$C
(credits 0.55~Mt, surplus \$716~M versus \$1{,}141~M canonically), and
mature-market production falls back to 2.30~Mt of which 1.59~Mt is
500$^\circ$C (surplus \$1{,}018~M versus \$1{,}501~M). Two conclusions follow.
First, the 300$^\circ$C exclusion is over-determined: the nearest measured
value is 0.9, two tenths above the gate, so no plausible H/C measurement error
re-qualifies torrefaction-like 300$^\circ$C chars. Second, the C2
technology-conversion result is robust within the published 500$^\circ$C H/C
band but degrades if agricultural-residue 500$^\circ$C chars were, contrary to
every measurement we found, above 0.7, which is why VM0044 and IBI treat the
gate as a per-batch measured property rather than a design assumption.

\subsection{Net-emission cap frontier (E2)}

The B2 sweep of the main text only tests caps at or above $-1{,}000$~kt, all
looser than the baseline net flux ($-1{,}595$~kt); non-binding there is built
into the experimental range. To locate where a net cap actually binds, we sweep
$\mathrm{CAP}^{\mathrm{net}}$ downward from $-1{,}600$ to $-2{,}200$~kt in
100-kt steps, twice: once on the fixed-layout LP and once on the
free-facility MIP (Fig.~\ref{fig:s2}; archived as
\texttt{netcap\_frontier\_fixedz\_v2.csv} and
\texttt{netcap\_frontier\_v2.csv}). The fixed-layout LP is feasible only at
$-1{,}600$~kt: the baseline layout installs zero 500$^\circ$C capacity and
saturates its 300$^\circ$C capacity at the H+M demand, so it cannot deepen its
net flux; its cap dual there is $-\$9{,}107$/t CO$_2$e (with the bracket
$[-\$9{,}107, 0]$ across a $\pm$50-kt perturbation, the degenerate vertex
interval of Section~A.9). The free-facility MIP, in contrast, responds along
the entire sweep. From $-1{,}600$ to $-1{,}800$~kt it deepens the net flux by
expanding production into the unprofitable L segment (2.30 to 2.61~Mt, all
300$^\circ$C) at a finite-difference marginal cost of $\approx$\$110/tCO$_2$e
(surplus falls from 708.5 to 686.0~M\$/yr). Below $-1{,}900$~kt the binding
cap mandates 500$^\circ$C conversion: 0.13~Mt at $-1{,}900$, 0.55~Mt at
$-2{,}000$, 0.78~Mt at $-2{,}100$, and 1.12~Mt at $-2{,}200$~kt, at marginal
costs of $\approx$\$150--330/tCO$_2$e (finite differences between TIME\_LIMIT
incumbents with 0.6--8.6\% gaps, so these point estimates are approximate).
The minimum feasible net flux is $N_{\min}=-2{,}602$~kt, attained by an
all-500$^\circ$C dispatch (2.26~Mt biochar), solved to optimality. A net cap
is therefore not blind to negative emissions: it binds once tightened below
the system's own flux, first as an expansion mandate and then as a durability
mandate, with shadow costs in the same order as gross-emission abatement. In the
mature-market scenario the expansion regime dominates the swept range: the
frontier is feasible to $-2{,}200$~kt with production expanding from 2.30 to
3.06~Mt (all 300$^\circ$C) at marginal costs of $\$14$--57/tCO$_2$e, and
$N_{\min}=-5{,}203$~kt (all-500$^\circ$C, 4.38~Mt, optimal), so durability
conversion would set in only deeper than $-2{,}200$~kt where feedstock
remains abundant (archived in the mature-market results directory).

\subsection{Policy decomposition: isolating the H/C gate (E1)}

Paradigms A, B, and C differ in more than one module at a time, so the policy
responses of Section~3 cannot be attributed to a single design element by
inspection. Table~S8 decomposes the six experiments by module (carbon basis,
eligibility gate, tax, cap instrument, allocation rule). The only module
combination not covered by the canonical runs is a sequestration-basis credit
\emph{without} the H/C gate; we re-solved Paradigm C2 on gate-off data
(every feedstock--temperature pair credit-eligible,
\texttt{biochar\_data\_v2\_gateoff}, tagged \texttt{policy\_C2\_gateoff\_v2.csv}).
Comparing the gate-on and gate-off C2 sweeps isolates the gate: at
$p_c=\$200$/t the gated system converts to 500$^\circ$C entirely (2.25~Mt,
zero 300$^\circ$C), while the gate-off system keeps 99\% of production on
300$^\circ$C (2.75 of 2.77~Mt) and earns only slightly more surplus
(\$1{,}184 vs.\ \$1{,}141~M/yr). The technology conversion reported in
Section~3.6 is therefore driven by the eligibility gate, not by the
sequestration credit basis alone; likewise, comparing A (throughput basis) with
the gate-off C2 (sequestration basis) isolates the credit-basis effect, and
comparing C1-free with C2 isolates the credit mechanism at fixed tax
(Table~\ref{tab:s8}).

\subsection{Demand-family sensitivity (E3)}

The calibrated demand curve is a scenario, not an estimated willingness-to-pay
surface; the \$25/t activation threshold and the claim that ``demand is the
binding constraint'' must therefore be tested against alternative demand
families. We re-solve the Step-1 MIP, the LP duals, and the Paradigm~A sweep
at $\{25, 100, 200\}$~\$/t for three variants (Fig.~\ref{fig:s4}; archived
under \texttt{results\_v2\_df*}): capacities $\times$0.7 (low adoption),
$\times$1.4 (high adoption), and a price stress (M \$450/t, L \$200/t). The
no-policy equilibrium follows the H+M capacity boundary (1.61 / 2.30 / 2.30 /
2.83~Mt), confirming that the aggregate output level is demand-determined. The
\$25/t activation of the L segment is scenario-conditional: 1.01~Mt under low
adoption (the smaller L capacity binds), 0.42~Mt calibrated, 0.12~Mt under
price stress (the lower \$200/t bid needs more credit support), and zero under
high adoption (the enlarged H+M capacity absorbs the profitable expansion and
the marginal cost of further L production exceeds \$250 plus \$25/t credits).
The \$25 threshold is thus a property of the calibrated demand, not a
universal policy constant; the paper's statements are bounded accordingly.

\subsection{Carbon-parameter joint scenarios (E4)}

The decomposition fraction and the permanence factors enter the credit rate
$R=B-E^{+}+|S|$ independently (decomposition scales $B$; permanence scales the
sequestration part of $|S|$). We re-solve the Paradigm~A point $p_c=\$200$/t
with free facilities on the $3\times3$ grid decomposition
$\{0.5,0.7,0.9\}\times$ permanence multipliers $\{0.8,1.0,1.2\}$
(archived as \texttt{policy\_A\_e4\_*\_v2.csv}; gaps 1.2--1.7\%). The baseline
cell (0.9, 1.0) reproduces the canonical \$200/t surplus to 0.1\%
(3{,}358 vs.\ 3{,}362~M\$/yr). Halving the decomposition fraction lowers the
\$200/t surplus by 31--34\% at every permanence level (\$2{,}203--2{,}451~M\$),
extending the one-at-a-time result of Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} to the
joint grid; a $\pm$20\% permanence perturbation moves the surplus by
$\mp$3--5\% (e.g., 3{,}224 / 3{,}358 / 3{,}473~M\$ at decomposition 0.9).
The near-term technology mix stays essentially all-300$^\circ$C in every cell
(at most 0.22~Mt of 500$^\circ$C at decomposition 0.9, permanence 1.2),
consistent with the supply-bound regime: carbon-parameter uncertainty scales
the credit economics but does not flip the near-term technology choice.

\subsection{Carbon ledger and cash-flow decomposition}

Table~S6 is the per-dry-tonne carbon ledger: carbon in (kg C/t dry), char
carbon at 300/500$^\circ$C, gas/liquid carbon (residual), process emissions
$E^{+}$, durable storage $|S|$ (sequestration plus N$_2$O), the avoided-decay
baseline $B$, the direct net flux $N=E^{+}-|S|$, and the credit rates of the
two bases (A: $B-E^{+}+|S|$; C2: $|S|$, H/C-gated). Table~S7 decomposes the
policy cash flows per point: consumer payment (demand-bid revenue), credit
revenue, tax payment, resource cost (farmgate, OPEX, transport, annualized
CAPEX), and the resulting surplus. Two identities close the ledger. First, at
the system level, the creditable quantity equals the baseline minus the net
flux, $\mathrm{cc}=B-N$ (e.g., 10.77~Mt $=$ 9.15~Mt $- (-1.60)$~Mt at
$p_c=0$; 13.56~Mt $=$ 11.57~Mt $- (-1.96)$~Mt at $p_c=\$200$/t; verified for
every sweep point to $<$0.05~Mt). This is why creditable quantity (up to
13.6~Mt) so far exceeds the physical net removal (1.60--1.96~Mt): the
throughput basis pays for the avoided baseline $B$, which is not a direct
atmospheric flux. Second, surplus equals consumer payment plus credit revenue
minus tax minus resource cost at every point (the decomposition columns sum
exactly). Credit revenue and tax are transfers between the operator and the
policy authority, not social welfare; the surplus figures of the main text
include them by definition and should be read as producer-side surpluses under
the policy instruments (Table~\ref{tab:s6}, Table~\ref{tab:s7}).

\subsection{Proof of Proposition 1}

For the fixed-layout LP, the cap constraint $E^{+}(x)\le\mathrm{CAP}$ is
appended to \eqref{eq:base} with the layout variables $z$ fixed. By the
standard parametric-LP sensitivity theorem (with strong duality per
Assumption~1),
$\mu_{\mathrm{CAP}}=\partial\Pi^{*}/\partial\,\mathrm{CAP}$, where
$\mu_{\mathrm{CAP}}$ is the optimal dual variable of the cap constraint; at
degenerate vertices the left and right derivatives can differ, and the dual
ranges reported at the end of this section are the basis for the reported
interval.
Gurobi reports the dual of a $\le$ constraint in a maximization with the
opposite sign convention (it reports the reduction in objective per unit of
constraint tightening), i.e., the reported value is
$-\partial\Pi^{*}/\partial\,\mathrm{CAP}$. The shadow carbon price
(marginal abatement cost) is therefore the negative of the reported dual, and
the finite-difference estimates
$\Delta\Pi^{*}/\Delta\,\mathrm{CAP}$ in Table~S3 reproduce it to numerical
tolerance ($-374$ vs. $\$351$--$391$~\$/t over the first binding intervals,
near-term).

\subsection{Proof of Proposition 2 (two-regime crossover)}

Fix feedstock $f$ and a biochar market price $P$, and consider the two
fixed-yield routes $r\in\{3,5\}$ with dry-biomass yields $\gamma^{r}_f$,
credit rates $\rho^{r}_f$ (tCC/t dry), and chain cost $\omega$ independent of
the route (farmgate is per dry tonne; the route-specific OPEX is already
absorbed in $\omega$; results are unchanged if route-specific costs
$\omega_3\ne\omega_5$ are used, with $\omega(1/\gamma^5-1/\gamma^3)$ replaced by
$\omega_5/\gamma^5-\omega_3/\gamma^3$).

\emph{Demand-bound.} When feedstock is abundant, the active constraint is the
demand bid: the model serves a fixed biochar quantity $Q$, and the supply path
with the higher surplus per tonne of biochar served wins. Per tonne served, the
route-$r$ chain consumes $1/\gamma^{r}_f$ dry tonnes, paying
$\omega/\gamma^{r}_f$ and earning credit revenue
$(\rho^{r}_f/\gamma^{r}_f)\,p_c$, so
\begin{equation}
\Pi^{r}/Q = P - \omega/\gamma^{r}_f + \rho^{r}_f\,p_c/\gamma^{r}_f .
\end{equation}
Subtracting the $r=3$ expression gives
$\Delta^{\mathrm{dem}}_f(p_c)$ as in the statement, which is affine in $p_c$
with slope $\rho^{5}_f/\gamma^{5}_f-\rho^{3}_f/\gamma^{3}_f>0$ (the 500$^\circ$C
credit rate per served tonne exceeds the 300$^\circ$C one; under the VM0044
gate $\rho^3_f=0$ this is automatic). It crosses zero at
$\bar{p}^{\mathrm{dem}}_f$, which is independent of $P$---$P$ cancels in the
difference---and positive whenever the slope is positive. The crossing is
unique because the difference is affine.

\emph{Supply-bound.} When feedstock is scarce, the active constraint is the
feedstock availability: a fixed dry mass $M$ is processed, and the route with
the higher surplus per dry tonne wins. Per dry tonne, route $r$ yields
$\gamma^{r}_f$ t biochar at price $P$ and earns credit revenue
$\rho^{r}_f\,p_c$, so
\begin{equation}
\Pi^{r}/M = \gamma^{r}_f P + \rho^{r}_f p_c - \omega .
\end{equation}
Subtracting gives
$\Delta^{\mathrm{sup}}_f(p_c)=(\gamma^{3}_f-\gamma^{5}_f)P-(\rho^{5}_f-\rho^{3}_f)p_c$,
affine in $p_c$ with \emph{negative} slope, crossing zero at
$\bar{p}^{\mathrm{sup}}_f$, strictly increasing in $P$ (the yield gap
$\gamma^3_f-\gamma^5_f>0$ multiplies $P$), and unique. Note the sign
asymmetry: in the demand-bound regime the credit slope is positive (500$^\circ$C
wins at high $p_c$ because its lower yield per dry tonne concentrates credit
revenue per served tonne), while in the supply-bound regime the credit slope
is negative (the yield loss is a direct revenue loss at price $P$, and credits
must compensate it per dry tonne).

\emph{Numerical values.} Using the pooled feedstock averages of the
LCFS-style baseline-and-credit rates adopted in Paradigm~A
($\bar{\gamma}^{3}=0.352$, $\bar{\gamma}^{5}=0.278$ t/t dry;
$\bar{\rho}^{3}=1.771$, $\bar{\rho}^{5}=1.849$ tCC/t dry, i.e.\ a
credit-rate premium of $\bar{\rho}^{5}-\bar{\rho}^{3}\approx 0.077$ tCC/t
dry; combined chain cost $\omega\approx\$130$/t dry) at the bulk-segment
price $P=\$250$/t:
\begin{equation}
\bar{p}^{\mathrm{dem}}
=\frac{130\,(1/0.278-1/0.352)}{1.849/0.278-1.771/0.352}
\approx\$61/\mathrm{t},\qquad
\bar{p}^{\mathrm{sup}}
=\frac{(0.352-0.278)\,250}{1.849-1.771}
\approx\$239/\mathrm{t}.
\end{equation}
Both values are confirmed by the paradigm-A sweep: the mature-market
(demand-bound) case switches to 500$^\circ$C at $p_c\approx\$61$/t and the
near-term (supply-bound) case stays on 300$^\circ$C through $p_c=\$200$/t with
crossover indicated near $p_c\approx\$239$/t (Fig.~\ref{fig:creditbasis},
which reports the same closed-form crossings with the feedstock averages).
$\square$

\subsection{Proof of Proposition 3 (tax invariance in the demand-constrained regime)}

Write the fixed-layout LP in the compact form
\begin{equation}
\Pi^{*}=\max_{x\in X}\,c^{\top}x,\qquad
X=\{x: Ax\le b,\ x\ge 0\},
\end{equation}
where the demand stack rows are $d\mapsto \mathrm{dem}_d\le \bar{d}_d$ with
objective coefficient $\lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}_d$ in $c$, and the mass-balance
rows carry duals $\pi$. Suppose $x^{*}$ is an optimal basic solution with the
property that the active demand rows are exactly those with
$\pi^{\mathrm{BC}}_{n}<\lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}_{d}$ (strict slack at the margin)
in the terminology of the statement---the demand-constrained regime. Consider
the perturbed problem
\begin{equation}
\Pi^{*}_{\tau}=\max_{x\in X}\,c^{\top}x-\tau\,e^{\top}x,
\quad e\ge 0 \text{ (gross-emission rates per activity unit)}.
\end{equation}
The perturbed objective is $(c-\tau e)^{\top}x$. We show
$\arg\max$ is unchanged. Write $x^{*}=\sum_{\text{basic } j} w_j x_j$; since
$x^{*}$ is an LP vertex with basis $B$, $x_B^{*}=B^{-1}b$ is determined by the
active constraints alone, so any perturbation of the objective---which does not
change the constraint matrix $A$---cannot change the vertex $x_B^{*}$; it can
only change \emph{whether} that vertex is optimal. Optimality persists iff the
reduced costs of the nonbasic activities $j$ remain nonpositive for the
current basis: $c^{\mathrm{red}}_j-\tau e^{\mathrm{red}}_j\le 0$ for all
nonbasic $j$. For the binding-demand rows, the reduced cost of the
corresponding supply path $q$ entering the basis is exactly
$c^{\mathrm{red}}_q=\lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}_{d(q)}-\pi^{\mathrm{BC}}_{n(q)}>0$
(the price gap at the margin); the path is already at its capacity bound and
entering more of it is blocked. All other nonbasic activities have
$c^{\mathrm{red}}_j\le 0$ (they are unprofitable at the current duals), so
they remain unattractive as long as
$\tau\,e^{\mathrm{red}}_j$ does not flip the sign of a positive reduced cost.
Because the only positive reduced costs are the marginal supply paths with
$\lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}-\pi^{\mathrm{BC}}>0$, the critical tax rate is
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\max}=\min_{q\ \text{marginal}}\frac{\lambda^{\mathrm{dem}}_{d(q)}-\pi^{\mathrm{BC}}_{n(q)}}{e^{+}_q}
\end{equation}
as stated; for $\tau<\tau_{\max}$ the optimal basis, and hence the material
flows, technology mix, and cleared quantities, are identical to $x^{*}$, while
the objective decreases by $\tau\,E^{+}(x^{*})$---the tax is a pure
surplus-to-government transfer. Numerically, the marginal price gap is
$\lambda^{\mathrm{M}}-\max_n\pi^{\mathrm{BC}}_n =
500-344.85\approx\$155$/t biochar. The route-specific marginal intensities
$e^{+}_q$ are evaluated from the LP basis by a $+1$~kt cap perturbation on each
fully-served demand row (\texttt{prop3\_basis\_check.jl}, archived as
\texttt{prop3\_basis\_check\_v2.csv}): the binding rows are the M-segment rows
served by the corn-stover 300$^\circ$C chain with
$e^{+}_q=0.475$--$0.488$~t CO$_2$e/t biochar (T1 dehydration, T2-300
pyrolysis, plus delivery), giving $\tau_q=0.32$~\$/kg CO$_2$e, i.e.,
$\tau_{\max}\approx\$321$/t CO$_2$e; H-segment rows give
$\approx$\$1{,}100/t. The threshold exceeds the top tested rate of
\$200/t, consistent with the null dispatch response of C1 and
C1-free (Table~\ref{tab:c1free}). The tiered slope does not matter: a
convex piecewise-linear tax with positive slopes is, on the active paths,
locally linear, so the argument applies tier by tier.
$\square$
